dwell:refuge — how this works

← back to the piece

The space

The piece is a house heard from inside. Four kinds of weather occupy the corners — storm, heat wave, flood, drought — and a refuge holds the center. Each is a room: anywhere inside it you hear that room at full presence; the seams between rooms sound like hallways, with the neighboring weather muffled as through a wall. Your cursor (or your device's tilt) is where you are listening.

How it listens back

  • Staying in a room intensifies it — not louder, but wronger: the AC compressor starts to sag and short-cycle, the leak drips faster, the wood complains more, the faucet gives less. After a few minutes the room settles again, below its peak. Chronic, not acute.
  • Being still opens the space: the filter lifts and detail emerges.
  • Walking away — after a while without input, your position begins to drift toward the nearest weather. The climate does not wait.
  • Somewhere in the refuge there is a radio. Now and then it airs a bulletin. You will recognize the cadence; you will not make out the words.

The engine

Everything runs on the plain Web Audio API in your browser — no frameworks, no server, no stream. The engine is hybrid, and honestly so: rain, thunder, wind, running water, night insects and small splashes are real field recordings of the present (credited below); everything else — the appliances, the drips, the creaks, the radio, the rooms' acoustics — is synthesized in real time and never plays the same way twice.

The house is tuned to its own infrastructure: the tonal center of every pitched sound is 50 Hz, the hum of the electrical grid. Each room owns a chord above it, so moving between rooms is also a slow harmonic modulation.

Lineage

The piece stands on named ground: Nick Foster's The Future Mundane (2013) — futures made of ordinary, partly broken things; Katie Paterson's Vatnajökull (the sound of) (2007) — the climate event arriving through a domestic device; Superflux's Mitigation of Shock (2017/2019) — the climate-adapted apartment, its radio bulletins and its calm; and Pierre Cusa's ambient.garden (2023) — composition organized in space rather than time.
